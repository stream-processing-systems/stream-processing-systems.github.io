Distributed processing and the log

How long does Kafka keep history?

How retention decides how far back a log can be read, why copies on several brokers protect against a different failure, and when keeping only the latest value of each key is enough.

Study with an AI assistant 🤖

Copy the prompt and paste it into a chat with an AI assistant.

On February 10, the team of our AI assistant fixes the bug introduced eight days earlier and wants to recompute usage. It restores the last checkpoint before the bug, from 06:01 on February 2. The usage topic uses Kafka’s default seven-day retention, and in this case the segments needed to continue from that checkpoint have already been deleted. The team can restore the saved state, but the log no longer has all the input needed to bring the totals up to date.

So the log keeps history only for a while, and that time is a setting. How long should records stay, what protects them while they stay, and does every topic need to keep every record?

Retention: how long records stay

Section titled “Retention: how long records stay”

How long a topic keeps its records is called retention. Kafka can limit it by time, by the size of each partition, or both. By default, a topic keeps records for seven days and has no size limit.1

Kafka stores each partition in files called segments and deletes old records a whole segment at a time. A segment goes once even its newest record is older than the retention time, and age is judged by the records’ timestamps.1 Usually, records stay somewhat longer than the setting, and the earliest available offset moves forward in jumps. It is not a promise, though: a record that arrives late with an old timestamp gets less time, and a size limit can remove segments earlier.

The right retention follows from what readers need. Our list, from the shortest need to the longest:

NeedHow far back the log must reach
Restart from a checkpointTo the oldest checkpoint the processor may restart from: minutes
Catch up after a processor outageThe longest outage we plan for: hours or days
Recompute after a bugAs long as a bug can go unnoticed: our bug went eight days
The finance team’s monthly checkThe whole month, plus the days the check takes after it ends

The longest need decides: about forty days. Storage grows with the rate of records. If the assistant writes 1,000 records a second on average, at about 300 bytes each, one day is about 26 GB, and forty days about 1 TB in each copy. That is affordable here. For older history, such as usage from last year that customers may dispute, Kafka can move old segments to cheaper remote storage, such as object storage, and keep only the recent ones on the brokers’ disks. This is called tiered storage.2

When a reader falls behind retention

Section titled “When a reader falls behind retention”

Retention deletes records whether or not someone has read them. Suppose the finance reader is stopped on February 3 for a migration and started again on February 11. Its committed offset points to records of February 3, and with seven-day retention those records are gone.

What happens next is a setting too. When a consumer’s offset no longer exists, Kafka moves it to the earliest or the latest available offset, and by default to the latest.3 The finance reader would quietly jump to the newest records and skip eight days. Its check would then compare part of the month with the billing system and find differences that are not real, or miss real ones.

A reader that must not skip records is better off with an error. With auto.offset.reset=none, the consumer reports an error instead of moving, and people decide what to do: find the records elsewhere or accept the gap knowingly.3

Copies on other brokers

Section titled “Copies on other brokers”

Retention protects against age. A failing disk is a different danger: if the broker that stores partition 0 loses its disk, a seven-day or a forty-day retention does not matter.

So Kafka keeps each partition on several brokers. One copy, on the leader, receives all writes; the other copies, the followers, read from the leader and repeat its writes. The leader together with the followers that keep up with it are called the in-sync replicas. With three copies, partition 0 might have its leader on broker-1 and followers on broker-2 and broker-3. If the leader fails, normally one of the in-sync followers becomes the new leader.4

When does a write count as stored? By default, the producer waits until all current in-sync replicas have the record.5 Kafka’s promise is that such a record is not lost as long as at least one in-sync replica stays alive.4 The word “current” matters. Suppose broker-2 and broker-3 fall behind and leave the in-sync set. The leader alone is now all the in-sync replicas, and it acknowledges writes by itself. If broker-1 then loses its disk, the only current copy is gone, and the records it acknowledged in that time are lost.

A topic can require a minimum number of in-sync replicas for a write. With the producer waiting for all of them and min.insync.replicas=2, the leader refuses writes while it is the only one in sync.1 The gateway then gets an error and keeps the record to send again, which is at-least-once delivery at work. The price is availability: while two of three brokers are down, the topic accepts no usage. The default, one, keeps accepting and risks the loss.4 For records that go into bills, we prefer the refusal.

Copies protect against broken machines, not against wrong data. Retention deletes a record on every copy at once. If the gateway writes wrong records, all three brokers keep them faithfully.

Keeping only the latest value

Section titled “Keeping only the latest value”

Not every topic needs its whole history. The usage processor publishes each account’s current total for the month to a topic account-totals, and the usage page reads it. Partition 0 of that topic receives:

OffsetKeyValue
0acme1,000
1acme1,400
2delta300
3acme2,000

Each new total replaces the previous one for its key. A page that starts reading this topic needs only the last value of each account. For such topics, Kafka offers compaction: instead of deleting records by age, it keeps at least the last record for each key and removes older records with the same key.4 After compaction, partition 0 holds delta 300 at offset 2 and acme 2,000 at offset 3. Offsets do not change, so a gap appears where the removed records were. A reader that reads the topic from the start still arrives at the latest value of every key.4

To remove a key completely, the producer writes a record with that key and a null value, no value at all, called a tombstone. An empty string would not do: compaction would keep it as the key’s latest value. Compaction removes the key’s earlier records, and the tombstone itself is removed later, by default after a day (delete.retention.ms). A reader that is away longer may never see that the key was deleted.4 The same holds for a reader that rebuilds the table from the start: it has to reach the end of the topic within that day, or it may read the last total of a closed account and miss its tombstone.

Compaction on the usage topic would be a mistake. Its key is the account too, but its records do not replace each other: each one adds tokens. After compaction, acme would keep only u-103 with 600 tokens, and a recomputed total would be 600 instead of 2,000. Records that add up need retention by time. Records that replace each other can be compacted. One system usually has both kinds of topics.

A compacted topic keeps the latest value of each key, which is what a table holds. The assistant already has a table of that kind, in a database: the plan of each account, with its price per token and monthly limit. The processor needs it to turn tokens into money, and the plan can change at any moment, when acme moves to a larger plan at 14:20. Copying the whole table now and then would miss the moment of the change and load the database. How can the changes made in a database become records in a log?

What to remember

Section titled “What to remember”
  • Retention decides how long a topic keeps records, by time or size, and Kafka deletes whole segments. It follows from the longest need of any reader.
  • A reader whose offset has been deleted by default jumps to the newest records. A reader that must not skip records should get an error instead.
  • Copies on several brokers protect against a broken machine. A minimum number of in-sync replicas trades availability for not losing acknowledged records. Copies do not protect against wrong data.
  • Compaction keeps the latest record of each key. It suits records that replace each other and breaks records that add up.
