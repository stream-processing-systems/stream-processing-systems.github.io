Distributed processing and the log

Consumer groups and replay in Kafka

How Kafka shares partitions among the consumers of a group and moves them when one fails, why a committed offset needs matching state, and how independent groups read the same history and read it again.

Study with an AI assistant 🤖

Copy the prompt and paste it into a chat with an AI assistant.

Three processors read the usage topic of our AI assistant: A reads partition 0, B reads 1, and C reads 2. At 11:00 on February 2, the machine of processor A fails. Partition 0, with the records of acme and delta, has no reader, and the usage pages of both accounts stop changing.

Kafka can notice this, hand partition 0 to another processor, and tell it where A stopped. Meanwhile, the finance team wants to read the same topic for its own check, and next week someone may want to count all of February again. How does Kafka decide who reads which partition, and what does a processor need, besides a position, to continue correctly?

A group shares the partitions

Section titled “A group shares the partitions”

Consumers that share the work of reading a topic form a consumer group. Each consumer names its group when it connects; ours is usage-totals. Kafka assigns each partition of the topic to exactly one consumer in the group.1 Together, the consumers of a group read every partition once, as one reader divided into parts:

Consumer in usage-totalsPartition
A0
B1
C2

A fourth consumer in this group would get no partition and wait, because one partition goes to one consumer of the group. This is the limit of partitions in Kafka’s terms.

Kafka also has another way to read. Since version 4.2, it offers share groups, in which several consumers read the same partition and acknowledge each record separately.2 That is the queue way of reading, on top of a log. It suits independent tasks; our totals need ordinary consumer groups, where all records of one key reach one consumer.

When a consumer leaves or joins

Section titled “When a consumer leaves or joins”

A consumer in a group regularly sends a signal that it is alive. If the signals stop for longer than a set timeout, Kafka considers the consumer dead and gives its partitions to the other consumers of the group. When a new consumer joins, Kafka moves some partitions to it.1 Moving partitions among the consumers of a group is called a rebalance.

With the default settings of Kafka’s Java client, a consumer is considered dead after 45 seconds without signals.3 So at about 11:00:45, partition 0 goes to B, say, and B now reads partitions 0 and 1. For those 45 seconds, the usage pages of acme and delta stand still, and then they catch up. A consumer also loses its partitions if it is alive but slow: if it does not ask for new records for five minutes, by default, it leaves the group.3 This is the slow processor that could keep running after losing its partition: once another consumer owns the partition, the old one must stop sending results for it.

Committed offsets

Section titled “Committed offsets”

B now needs a place in partition 0 to start from. A’s position lived in A’s memory and is gone. For this reason, the group stores its progress in Kafka: for each partition, the offset of the next record to read. Writing this offset to Kafka is called a commit, and the stored value is the committed offset.1 B starts reading partition 0 from the offset that A committed last.

When to commit decides which failure is possible. A consumer that commits before processing the records may lose them: if it fails in between, the new owner starts after records that nobody processed. A consumer that commits after processing may process them twice: if it fails in between, the new owner reads them again.4 These are the at-most-once and at-least-once guarantees, now on the stretch between the log and the processor.

By default, the Java client commits automatically, every five seconds, the offsets of the records that poll has returned.3 That suits a consumer that finishes each batch before asking for the next one. A consumer that hands records to other threads and polls again can commit offsets for records that are still being processed, and a failure then loses them.1

An offset without the state

Section titled “An offset without the state”

Suppose A committed offset 4 before it failed: it had counted the records at offsets 0 to 3, and acme’s total in its memory was 1,400 tokens. B starts at offset 4 and reads u-103, 600 tokens. But B has no state for acme, so its total for the account is 600, not 2,000. The committed offset carried A’s position to B. A’s total was in A’s memory and failed with it.

A committed offset is reading progress and nothing more. The new owner of a partition also needs the state that matches that offset: the state that includes exactly the records before it, as in a consistent checkpoint. Kafka’s consumer cannot provide this, because it does not know what the processor keeps in memory.

There are two common ways to keep the offset and the state together. The processor can store the offset with its results, for example in the same database transaction, and start from that stored offset instead of the committed one.1 Or it can keep the offsets in its own checkpoints, next to its state. Flink does the latter: its Kafka source restores offsets from the checkpoint, does not rely on committed offsets for recovery, and commits them only so that others can see its progress.5 In this example, the saved state is from before the copy at offset 3. Either way, B restores acme with 1,400 tokens and offset 3, recognizes the copy of u-102, counts u-103, and reaches 2,000.

Independent readers

Section titled “Independent readers”

The finance team starts its own consumer with its own group, finance-check. Kafka keeps committed offsets for each group separately, so the two groups read the same partitions without affecting each other. On the evening of February 5, their offsets look like this:

GroupPartition 0Partition 1Partition 2
usage-totals251,408,377248,915,102250,301,977
finance-check64,120,55063,498,33163,905,720

The usage processors are near the newest records. The finance reader, which started from the earliest records, is far behind and moves at its own pace. Neither group’s progress deletes anything.

A new group has no committed offsets yet. By default, its consumers start at the end of each partition and read only records that arrive after they connect.3 The finance reader needs the opposite, so it is configured to start from the earliest available offset.

Replay on purpose

Section titled “Replay on purpose”

On February 10, the team finds a bug. Since the update at 06:05 on February 2, the processor has also counted records with status failed: requests that ended in an error, which customers do not pay for. The records carry the status, so a fixed processor can tell them apart. After the fix, everything since the update must be counted again.

The replay has to start from a state and offsets that match. The last checkpoint before the update, from 06:01, holds both: the totals and the lists of counted IDs, and for each partition the offset up to which they are counted. The processor restores that checkpoint, if it is still kept, and reads each partition from the checkpoint’s offset. A processor that keeps offsets in its checkpoints, as Flink does, does this by itself. For our group, we stop all usage-totals consumers and restore their state. While the group is inactive, we reset its offsets with Kafka’s command-line tool, from a file that lists the topic, partition, and offset for each partition:6

Then we start the consumers with the fixed code and the restored state.

The same tool can move offsets to a time instead. It then finds the first records whose timestamps are after that time, and a timestamp says nothing about which records a checkpoint included. If at 06:05 the processor was 20 minutes behind, as it is at the morning peak, its state ended at records written around 05:45. A reset by time suits a reader that has no state to match.

Without a checkpoint from before the update, the totals have to be counted from the first record of the month. If the current totals stayed, every request since the update would be added a second time, and a list of IDs kept for 24 hours would not recognize records from last week.

The receivers need a rule for the recomputed results too. The usage page keeps the highest version of each total, and a version is the position of the last record the total includes. The recomputed totals carry the same positions as the old ones, so the page would reject them as old. The recomputation needs versions of its own, for example a run number placed before the position, so that every total of the second run is newer than any total of the first. The billing system, which treats a known usage ID with a different token count as an error, needs an explicit correction. Replay recomputes the results; what a recomputed result means is up to each receiver.

Replay also needs the records themselves. By default, Kafka keeps the records of a topic for seven days.7 By February 10, eight days have passed since the bug appeared. With the default retention, the segments holding the start of that period may already be deleted. The log would then lack part of the input needed to recompute the totals.

What to remember

Section titled “What to remember”
  • A consumer group shares a topic’s partitions: each partition goes to one consumer of the group. When a consumer fails or joins, a rebalance moves partitions.
  • The committed offset is the group’s progress, stored in Kafka. Committing before processing risks loss, committing after it risks repeats.
  • A committed offset carries no state. The new owner of a partition needs state that matches the offset, so offsets are kept with the state or with the results.
  • Different groups read the same topic independently. Replay starts from the offsets of a checkpoint together with its state, and receivers need a rule for recomputed results.
