Time and windows

How far has the stream progressed?

What a watermark tells a processor about event-time progress, and why it is an estimate.

Study with an AI assistant 🤖

Copy the prompt and paste it into a chat with an AI assistant.

The map for electric-car drivers groups each charger’s readings into session windows with a ten-minute gap. For charger charger-7, the last reading of the first session came at 09:09 by event time, so the session ends at 09:19, once ten minutes pass without a reading. Then the map can stop showing the charger as in use.

But ten minutes of what? The readings travel to the map’s processor with delays, so when its own clock shows 09:19, a reading from 09:15 may still be on its way. When can the processor decide that event time has passed 09:19?

Neither the clock nor the latest event

Section titled “Neither the clock nor the latest event”

The processor’s own clock does not answer this. As we saw with event time and processing time, the gap between the two changes from record to record. If the feed slows down for a minute, everything arrives a minute later, and windows closed by the processor’s clock lose their records.

The latest event time seen so far does not answer it either. A reading from 09:21 shows that it is at least 09:21 somewhere, but readings from 09:20 can still arrive after it. Readings from many chargers share one feed, and they do not arrive in the order they were taken.

The processor needs a separate signal of progress in event time: a statement that earlier records are no longer expected.

A watermark

Section titled “A watermark”

That signal is called a watermark. A watermark of 09:19 says: readings with event times before 09:19 are no longer expected.1 Once the watermark passes the end of a window, the processor can treat the window as over. Engines differ on whether a record exactly at the watermark time still counts. Our examples avoid such records.

Someone has to produce the watermark. Suppose the map knows its feed well: readings almost never arrive more than two minutes out of order. Then the processor can keep the watermark two minutes behind the latest event time it has seen.2 Readings from other chargers move it forward too, because the watermark belongs to the feed, not to one charger.

The reading from 09:16 arrived eight minutes out of order, more than the two minutes the rule allowed for. The watermark said that earlier readings were no longer expected, and one came anyway. Records that arrive with event times before the current watermark are called late.1

So a watermark is an estimate. If the feed is a file of yesterday’s readings sorted by time, the source knows that nothing earlier will follow, and its watermark can be exact. A live feed from chargers on mobile connections cannot know that. Its watermark is a guess based on how the feed usually behaves, and a reading stuck on a weak connection can prove it wrong.

How far behind to stay

Section titled “How far behind to stay”

The two-minute allowance is a choice, and it is the same trade-off as holding events: waiting longer against being wrong more often. With a ten-minute allowance, the watermark would have been at 09:14 when the 09:16 reading arrived, so the reading would have come in time, and session 1 would have merged with session 2 as it should. But the processor would then need readings eight minutes further ahead in event time before ending any session. How much longer drivers would see a charger as busy depends on when such readings arrive.

The difference from holding events is the clock. The hold was measured on the processor’s clock. The watermark is measured in event time, by the readings themselves. If the map runs yesterday’s readings through the processor again, it may process an hour of readings in a minute. The watermark moves through that hour as fast as the readings do: windows end when the readings say so, not when the processor’s clock does. To get exactly the same results as the first time, the records also have to arrive in the same order.

Several inputs

Section titled “Several inputs”

The map gets readings from two feeds: the city’s own chargers come through feed A, and a partner network’s chargers through feed B. Each feed has its own watermark. Some results use readings from both, such as the number of chargers in use across the city in each ten-minute window. A window from 09:10 to 09:20 of that count can end only when neither feed can still send a reading from before 09:20.

So a processor that reads several inputs takes the smallest of their watermarks as its own.3

InputWatermark
Feed A09:23
Feed B09:17
The processor09:17

Feed A has moved past 09:20, but feed B can still send readings from 09:17 to 09:20, and they belong to the window. The slower feed holds back every window the processor computes, including charger-7’s session, even though charger-7 reports through feed A.

An input that falls silent

Section titled “An input that falls silent”

Late at night, all the partner’s chargers stand unused, and feed B sends nothing. With no readings, its watermark stops moving, and so does the processor’s. Feed A keeps reporting, but no window ends: a charger on feed A that went quiet an hour ago still shows as in use.

To get unstuck, the processor can mark an input as idle after some time without records, say one minute, and leave it out of the smallest watermark until it sends something again.4 That minute is measured on the processor’s clock, because feed B’s event time is exactly what stopped moving.

Marking feed B idle lets the windows end. It does not prove that feed B has nothing left to send. Its chargers may be unused, or the feed may be down, and silence looks the same in both cases. If feed B comes back with readings taken before the processor’s watermark, those readings are late.

The watermark gives the processor a moment to act: at 09:19 by event time, session 1 is over. It does not say what to do around that moment. The map could show a session’s progress before the watermark reaches its end, rather than keep drivers waiting. And when the late reading from 09:16 arrived, the session it belonged to was already over. Should the processor still correct it, and for how long should it keep the state it needs for that?

What to remember

Section titled “What to remember”
  • A watermark is a signal of progress in event time: records from before it are no longer expected. A window can be treated as over once the watermark passes its end.
  • A watermark that trails the latest event time by an allowance is an estimate. A record that arrives behind it is late.
  • A larger allowance means fewer late records and later results. The watermark moves with the event time of the records, not with the processor’s clock.
  • A processor with several inputs uses the smallest of their watermarks. An idle input can be left out, but its later records may arrive late.
